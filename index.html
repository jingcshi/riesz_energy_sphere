The simulation minimizes E(x) = ∑i<j ||xi − xj||−p over N points constrained to the unit sphere, via projected gradient descent.

p=1 with the Euclidean metric is ordinary Coulomb/Newtonian potential — the natural law for points that repel through our physical 3D space. p=0 is the logarithmic energy −ln(d), the natural law for a genuinely 2-dimensional universe with no interior to radiate a field through — the classic Fekete-point problem.

The Spherical metric switches the distance d in that formula from the Euclidean chord to the geodesic (great-circle) angle, matching a universe where nothing can act except along the sphere's own surface. The metric toggle is independent of edge rendering below.

The N slider steps one point at a time to 64, then doubles its step at each power of two up to 1024. Nearly everything worth stepping through one point at a time — the magic numbers, the defect patterns, the N=5 degeneracy — is at small N, whereas past a few hundred a single extra point changes nothing visible. Type into the readout beside the label for an exact N the slider skips.

The p slider runs from 0 to 1000 on a coarsening scale — fine enough near p≈15 to resolve the known triangular-bipyramid→square-pyramid transition at N=5, then geometric out to the high exponents — plus a literal ∞ at the very top for the Tammes problem (maximizing the closest pairwise distance) that the energy sum degenerates into as p grows without bound. High p works because the energy is minimized in log form: each pair's contribution is measured relative to the closest pair's, so nothing overflows however large p gets, and the objective (1/p)·log E tends to −log(min separation) — i.e. large p already is an approximate Tammes solver, accurate to about log(pairs)/p. At p=1000 the resulting separations sit within 0.03° of the rigorously known optima for N=4–9, 12 and 24. ∞ itself isn't yet wired up to drive the simulation (Play is disabled for it).

Energy is the quantity being minimized. It switches to its natural logarithm once the energy itself outgrows what double precision can represent, which happens well before the top of the p slider.

Max force is the largest net force on any single vertex — the most physically direct reading, but not a portable one, because the force's own units grow roughly like ep. Above p=64 even that overflows, so it switches to dimensionless relative units.

Residual is the scale-free alternative: the largest per-vertex gradient of (1/p)·log E, the objective actually being descended. It means the same thing at every p, which is why it and not max force is the charted convergence measure. The two coincide at p=0, and agree to within rounding above p=64 where max force is already normalized; in between, max force spans some 26 decades at p=25 and plateaus around 1e+3 on a converged run, which reads as a stall when nothing is stalling.

Min separation is the angular distance of the closest pair. It's the quantity the Tammes problem maximizes, and what large p converges toward.

Each point is coloured by its current tension, from pale (settled) through mint, yellow, and orange to red (still moving fast), fading toward the background blue with depth. Tension is measured relative to the most this configuration has ever held, over four decades — so red means "as tense as it ever was" and pale means "at least ten thousand times quieter". It has to be a ratio rather than a raw force, because the force's own scale grows roughly like ep: at p=25 every vertex carries a net force around 1e+8 even once fully converged, which as an absolute number would read as permanently red. While paused, hovering a front-facing vertex or edge shows its numeric details.

Clicking a "Degree x" row in the statistics panel's histogram cycles it through three states: normal, highlighted (rings every vertex of that degree in yellow), and hidden (removes them from view entirely, display-only — the simulation underneath is unaffected).

Hiding is more than erasure: with anything hidden, both the edge and face layers are recomputed over just the surviving points and the extra structure that reveals is drawn dashed. Keep only a defect degree (say the 12 isolated 5-fold points on a large otherwise-6-fold mesh) and you get their own triangulation, showing how they sit relative to each other, rather than a lattice with holes punched in it. Dashed doesn't mean less real: the underlying potential has no notion of a triangulation at all, so a dashed "non-local" edge is an ordinary interacting pair and hovering it reports its force like any other.

Edges come from the 3D convex hull of the points — since every point sits exactly on the unit sphere, the hull's triangular faces are the spherical Delaunay triangulation, independent of the metric setting.

A raw hull is always triangles, so a genuine square face (e.g. the square antiprism's top and bottom at N=8) comes back as two triangles split by an arbitrary diagonal. Each vertex therefore drops any incident hull edge longer than 1.35× its own shortest edge, which erases exactly that spurious diagonal without touching real edges.

That 1.35 has hard limits either side. A square's diagonal is always exactly √2 ≈ 1.4142 times its side, whatever p or N, so the constant has to stay below √2 or the diagonals it exists to remove survive; and it has to stay above about 1.25, the largest ratio a genuine edge is known to reach, or real edges start disappearing. Filtered edges (see V, E, F, χ) are the cost of the remaining margin.

Faces are a separate, purely visual layer: adjacent hull triangles whose outward normals are nearly parallel (within ~1.8°) are merged into a single flat n-gon, named Triangle/Quadrilateral/ Pentagon/… up to Icosagon (20 sides) and "N-gon" beyond, colour-coded consistently across the app.

Early in a run, or for a generically asymmetric configuration, adjacent triangles almost never share an exact plane, so nothing merges — genuine flat faces (if the configuration has any) only appear once the system has relaxed close enough to a symmetric equilibrium. Hovering an edge reports the dihedral angle of the two faces meeting along it, which is the very quantity that test measures: 180° is flat, and anything within ~1.8° of it would have merged.

The left panel's Faces Hide/Show control is the master switch for the whole layer, hidden by default so the generic triangle soup stays out of the way until asked for. The statistics panel's "Faces by side count" list is finer-grained, letting you suppress individual side counts (e.g. hide triangles but keep pentagons) once the master switch is on. That per-side-count choice persists independently, so toggling Hide/Show never forgets which categories you'd already dismissed.

Hiding vertices affects faces the same way it affects edges: a face touching a hidden vertex disappears, and the merge is rerun over just the remaining visible points, revealing dashed "non-local" faces — a tiling of just the visible subset that wouldn't exist while the rest of the mesh was still there to subdivide it.

The Geometry table counts the vertices, edges and faces currently described, and their Euler characteristic V − E + F. Any closed surface gives χ=2, so it doubles as a check that the two layers agree. Everything in it follows hiding: a non-local edge or face belongs to the visible subset's own triangulation just as legitimately as a local one does, so it counts. Below four visible points there's no hull, no closed surface, and χ falls short of 2 accordingly.

E is the face tiling's edge count, and an amber +n beside it means n of those edges were rejected by the 1.35× filter and so aren't drawn. Nothing is wrong when this appears: the edge and face layers apply independent tests (a length ratio versus a coplanarity tolerance) and can reach different verdicts about a single edge. Counting only the accepted ones would push χ above 2 and suggest torn topology on a surface that is in fact perfectly closed, so the table names the discrepancy instead of hiding it.

It is most visible during the chaotic early transient, where a vertex's shortest edge swings around enough for the ratio test to cut aggressively; a converged configuration typically has none at all, and a handful at most.

Drag on the sphere to rotate the view. The zoom slider, top right of the canvas, scales the view about its centre only, with no panning.

Below it, sphere opacity treats the sphere as a ball of absorbing medium and reads as the fraction of light it absorbs across one full diameter. Fully transparent shows everything equally, front and back, which hides nothing but makes the layering illegible. Fully opaque blocks the entire rear hemisphere, as an opaque ball does — so geometry crossing the silhouette simply stops there. In between, anything behind the sphere dims exponentially with how much of the ball the light had to cross to reach you, which is why a point just inside the rim stays legible long after the antipode has gone.

The front hemisphere has nothing in front of it, so strictly it shouldn't dim at all. It's faded anyway, by the same slider: that's aerial perspective rather than optics, and without it the near geometry loses all sense of depth.

The Shape control decides how both the edge and face layers are drawn, whichever of them is shown. Chords gives straight edges and flat polygon faces — the inscribed polytope. Arcs gives great-circle edges and spherical patch faces, so a shown face reads as a genuine tile on the sphere's own surface. It's independent of the Metric setting, which governs the physics rather than the drawing. Hover measurements follow it, so an edge's length and a face's circumference and area always describe the surface you're actually looking at.

N, p, seed, and animation speed can all be changed live, whether paused or playing. Reset re-seeds the initial configuration from a fresh random draw using the current N and seed.
