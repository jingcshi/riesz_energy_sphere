The simulation minimizes E(x) = ∑i<j ||xi − xj||−p over N points constrained to the unit sphere, via projected gradient descent.

p=1 with the Euclidean metric is ordinary Coulomb/Newtonian potential — the natural law for points that repel through our physical 3D space. p=0 is the logarithmic energy −ln(d), the natural law for a genuinely 2-dimensional universe with no interior to radiate a field through — the classic Fekete-point problem.

The Spherical metric switches the distance d in that formula from the Euclidean chord to the geodesic (great-circle) angle, matching a universe where nothing can act except along the sphere's own surface. The metric toggle is independent of edge rendering below.

The p slider runs from 0 to 25 on a coarsening scale — fine enough near p≈15 to resolve the known triangular-bipyramid→square-pyramid transition at N=5 — plus a literal ∞ at the very top for the Tammes problem (maximizing the closest pairwise distance) that the energy sum degenerates into as p grows without bound. The slider stops at 25 rather than climbing further because floating-point precision in the force calculation is already becoming unreliable there; ∞ itself isn't yet wired up to drive the simulation (Play is disabled for it).

Edges come from the 3D convex hull of the points — since every point sits exactly on the unit sphere, the hull's triangular faces are the spherical Delaunay triangulation, independent of the metric setting above.

A raw hull is always triangles, so a genuine square face (e.g. the square antiprism's top and bottom at N=8) comes back as two triangles split by an arbitrary diagonal. Each vertex drops any incident hull edge longer than 1.3× its own shortest edge, which erases exactly that spurious diagonal without touching real edges.

Drag on the sphere to rotate the view; the zoom slider (top right of the canvas) scales the view about its centre only, with no panning.

N, p, seed, and animation speed can all be changed live, whether paused or playing. Reset re-seeds the initial configuration from a fresh random draw using the current N and seed.

Each point is coloured by its current net force, from pale (settled) through mint, yellow, and orange to red (still moving fast), fading toward the background blue with depth. While paused, hovering a front-facing vertex or edge shows its numeric details.

Clicking a "Degree x" row in the statistics panel's histogram cycles it through three states: normal, highlighted (rings every vertex of that degree in yellow), and hidden (removes them from view entirely, display-only — the simulation underneath is unaffected). Hiding everything but a defect degree (e.g. the 12 isolated 5-fold points on a large, otherwise 6-fold relaxed mesh) also reveals a second, dashed set of edges: the triangulation of just the points still visible, so their own global arrangement becomes visible instead of a lattice with holes in it.
