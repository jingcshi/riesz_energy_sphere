The simulation minimizes E(x) = ∑i<j ||xi − xj||−p over N points constrained to the unit sphere, via projected gradient descent.

p=1 with the Euclidean metric is ordinary Coulomb/Newtonian potential — the natural law for points that repel through our physical 3D space. p=0 is the logarithmic energy −ln(d), the natural law for a genuinely 2-dimensional universe with no interior to radiate a field through — the classic Fekete-point problem.

The Spherical metric switches the distance d in that formula from the Euclidean chord to the geodesic (great-circle) angle, matching a universe where nothing can act except along the sphere's own surface. The metric toggle is independent of edge rendering below.

The p slider runs from 0 to 1000 on a coarsening scale — fine enough near p≈15 to resolve the known triangular-bipyramid→square-pyramid transition at N=5, then geometric out to the high exponents — plus a literal ∞ at the very top for the Tammes problem (maximizing the closest pairwise distance) that the energy sum degenerates into as p grows without bound. High p works because the energy is minimized in log form: each pair's contribution is measured relative to the closest pair's, so nothing overflows however large p gets, and the objective (1/p)·log E tends to −log(min separation) — i.e. large p already is an approximate Tammes solver, accurate to about log(pairs)/p. At p=1000 the resulting separations sit within 0.03° of the rigorously known optima for N=4–9, 12 and 24. ∞ itself isn't yet wired up to drive the simulation (Play is disabled for it).

Edges come from the 3D convex hull of the points — since every point sits exactly on the unit sphere, the hull's triangular faces are the spherical Delaunay triangulation, independent of the metric setting above.

A raw hull is always triangles, so a genuine square face (e.g. the square antiprism's top and bottom at N=8) comes back as two triangles split by an arbitrary diagonal. Each vertex drops any incident hull edge longer than 1.3× its own shortest edge, which erases exactly that spurious diagonal without touching real edges.

Drag on the sphere to rotate the view; the zoom slider (top right of the canvas) scales the view about its centre only, with no panning.

N, p, seed, and animation speed can all be changed live, whether paused or playing. Reset re-seeds the initial configuration from a fresh random draw using the current N and seed.

Each point is coloured by its current net force, from pale (settled) through mint, yellow, and orange to red (still moving fast), fading toward the background blue with depth. While paused, hovering a front-facing vertex or edge shows its numeric details.

Clicking a "Degree x" row in the statistics panel's histogram cycles it through three states: normal, highlighted (rings every vertex of that degree in yellow), and hidden (removes them from view entirely, display-only — the simulation underneath is unaffected). Hiding everything but a defect degree (e.g. the 12 isolated 5-fold points on a large, otherwise 6-fold relaxed mesh) also reveals a second, dashed set of edges: the triangulation of just the points still visible, so their own global arrangement becomes visible instead of a lattice with holes in it. These "non-local" edges are just as real and physically interacting as any other — they're simply not part of the Delaunay triangulation over the currently- visible points — so hovering one reports its force exactly like any other edge.

Faces are a separate, purely visual layer: adjacent hull triangles whose outward normals are nearly parallel (within ~1.8°) are merged into a single flat n-gon, named Triangle/Quadrilateral/ Pentagon/… up to Icosagon (20 sides) and "N-gon" beyond, colour-coded consistently across the app. The left panel's Faces Hide/Show control is the master switch for the whole layer (hidden by default, so the generic triangle soup stays out of the way until asked for); the statistics panel's "Faces by side count" list is finer-grained, letting you dim out individual side counts (e.g. hide triangles but keep pentagons) once the master switch is on. That per-side-count choice persists independently of the master switch, so toggling Hide/Show never forgets which categories you'd already suppressed. In "Arcs" edge style, face boundaries are drawn as great-circle arcs too, so a shown face reads as a genuine tile on the sphere's surface rather than a flat polytope facet; in "Lines"/"None" style they're drawn as flat polygons. Early in a run, or for a generically asymmetric configuration, adjacent triangles almost never share an exact plane, so nothing merges — genuine flat faces (if the configuration has any) only appear once the system has relaxed close enough to a symmetric equilibrium.

Hiding vertices affects faces the same way it affects edges: a face touching a hidden vertex disappears, and the merge is rerun over just the remaining visible points, revealing dashed "non-local" faces — a tiling of just the visible subset that wouldn't exist while the rest of the mesh was still there to subdivide it.
